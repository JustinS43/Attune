Attune build plan

How to build the Attune prototype end to end: a webcam and MLH Hardware Lab sensors taped to your glasses, and free AI software running entirely on your laptop. The prototype puts captions on the right faces, learns names, translates, and turns doorbells and smoke alarms into lights and buzzes. Nothing here is built yet; every step says how to check that it works.

Section 01

Key decisions

These nine choices shape everything below. Each one came out of research this week, or out of a test on this laptop.

  1. The laptop is the brain; the glasses only sense and signal. The rig sees, hears, flashes, buzzes and takes a tap. All thinking happens on the laptop's GPU, and no internet is needed.
  2. One Python engine owns the camera, the microphone and the Arduino. The browser pages only draw what the engine tells them: the lens view and the operator console, both on the laptop. There is no phone in the system. Only one program can safely hold a webcam, and this keeps timing in one place.
  3. Faces come from InsightFace, not MediaPipe. We measured MediaPipe's built-in face finder: it only reliably sees faces within about 1 m Verified. InsightFace's SCRFD detector, run on the full 1080p frame, is built to find small faces, so it should reach 3–4 m Researched. MediaPipe stays, but only to read lip movement on each cropped face.
  4. Captions use NVIDIA's Nemotron 3.5 streaming speech model. It transcribes while you speak, adds punctuation and names the language. It runs natively on Windows through sherpa-onnx's official ONNX build, with faster-whisper as the fallback. Researched
  5. "Who's talking" combines lips, voice and loudness.
    • Visible speaker: the face whose lips move while speech is heard. The rule uses how much the lip opening varies over a rolling 1-second window. We measured it on the film's video clips. Verified
    • "You": the wearer's own voice is recognized by how loud it is at the two sound sensors on the glasses, about 10 cm from their mouth.
    • Off-screen speakers: placed left or right by comparing the two sensors.
  6. One local language model does names, translation and descriptions. The Qwen 3.5 model already installed in Ollama returns names as structured data. It translates, and it describes a stranger's clothing. Using one model keeps GPU memory under control. A tap on the touch sensor confirms any name before it's used.
  7. Two independent checks for alarms. A sound-recognition model (EfficientAT) runs alongside a detector for the standard smoke-alarm rhythm of three beeps and a pause. Together they give alerts that are both fast and hard to fool. The LEDs and motor then signal the alarm on the side it came from.
  8. Consent first, and people's data stays on the laptop. Only people who agree are enrolled. Strangers are remembered for the current session only and are described by their clothing, never their body. There's a switch to forget the session. The only thing that goes online is a reply the wearer types for ElevenLabs to speak.
  9. ElevenLabs speaks for the wearer, and the laptop keeps a day of history. ElevenLabs speaks replies the wearer types, through the laptop's speakers, which makes Attune two-way. A local SQLite file stores the conversation history for 24 hours, so the wearer can scroll back through what was said; then it deletes itself.

Changed since the CAD page

The first version of the CAD page described a browser app that read the camera and serial port itself (both pages now match). This plan moves that work into a Python engine for two reasons. The strongest free models (InsightFace, Nemotron, EfficientAT) run on the GPU from Python. And one owner for every device keeps audio, video and sensor timing on one clock. The rig, the parts and the pin map stay the same.

Where each job runs

The engine is one process with several worker threads. Heavy maths runs in native GPU libraries, so Python's single-thread limit doesn't bottleneck it. If the lens view drops below 24 fps or captions start lagging, move the face workers into a second process. That is the first fix to reach for.

What the Arduino does

The firmware is small and dependable, and it makes no decisions about people or sounds; the laptop does. It measures, reports, and plays patterns with exact timing.

Sound levels

Reads both sound sensors thousands of times a second. Every 50 ms it reports each side's loudness: the gap between the highest and lowest reading, because the sensor outputs raw audio. It marks readings taken while the motor runs, since the motor adds noise.

Touch gestures

Tap (under 0.4 s) means yes. Hold (0.8 s or more) means no. A double tap (two taps within 0.4 s) pauses or resumes recognition. The sensor calibrates itself at power-up, so don't touch it while plugging in.

Lights and buzz

Plays named patterns from the table below, so the rhythm stays exact even if the laptop is busy. The motor starts with a 40 ms soft ramp, and no single buzz lasts longer than 0.6 s.

Safety stop

If the laptop sends nothing for 2 s, all lights and the motor stop. The motor never runs more than 60% of any 2 seconds.

Status display

The board's built-in 12×8 LED matrix shows a heartbeat when linked, "!" and an arrow during an alert, "P" when paused and "?" when the laptop link is lost. It's handy for debugging on your chest.

Start-up

Looks for the driver on the I²C bus (0x14 or 0x0F), then announces itself with its version and the driver it found. The board's Wi-Fi stays off; USB is simpler and faster.

Capture

  • Open the webcam by name rather than number, because the built-in camera is usually camera 0. Ask for MJPG at 1920×1080 and 30 fps, with a one-frame buffer so frames never queue up.
  • Stamp every frame and every audio block with the same clock on arrival. USB video usually trails audio by 50–100 ms; a clap test measures the exact offset (see calibration).
  • Use the webcam's own microphone. It sits on the glasses, like a real product's would. Turn off Windows audio enhancements for it, and set the input level so your own voice peaks around −6 dBFS.
  • Resample once into two streams: 16 kHz for speech and 32 kHz for the alert model.
  • Keep the last 30 s of audio and 5 s of frames in memory. The voice-print and description jobs read from them, and replay mode records them.
  • If the webcam disappears, switch to the laptop's built-in mic and show "Camera lost" while captions keep running.

Faces: find, track, name

  • Find. SCRFD runs on every frame, starting at a 960-pixel detection size; T-S3 picks between 640, 960 and 1280 by speed and range. Faces under 36 px wide are ignored.
  • Track. Each new box is matched to an existing track by overlap, using where that track was heading. A track survives 1 s without a detection, for someone turning away, then moves to a 10 s "lost" list. If the same face returns within 10 s, it gets its old track and name back.
  • Remember exits. When a track is lost within 10% of the left or right edge, the engine notes "left the frame on the left" and the time. This drives the off-screen arrow.
  • Name. ArcFace turns a straightened 112×112 face crop into a 512-number face print. Only good crops count: at least 60 px wide, roughly facing the camera, and not blurred.
  • Match rules. A name appears only when the best match scores ≥ 0.45 and beats the runner-up by ≥ 0.08, three times within 1 s; the 0.45 is tuned in T-S3. Named faces are rechecked every 2 s, and after three failed rechecks the tag returns to "unknown". A tag never jumps straight from one name to another, so a wrong name is much rarer than an "unknown".
  • Nothing else is inferred. The buffalo_l pack also contains an age and gender model. It is never loaded.

Enrolling someone, with consent

  1. The operator presses Enroll in the console and picks the person's face from live thumbnails.
  2. The person reads a one-line notice: "Attune stores a face print and a voice print on this laptop to show your name. You can delete them anytime." They tick consent themselves, and the console records their name and the time.
  3. They face the webcam from about 1 m and turn their head a little left and right for 5 s. The engine keeps the 8 most varied good crops. With fewer than 5 it refuses and says why ("more light" or "come closer").
  4. They talk for 5 s. Silero trims the silence and CAM++ stores a voice print.
  5. Their tag switches to their name at once. The console lists everyone enrolled, with Rename and Delete; Delete removes every file for that person.

Who's talking

While speech is detected, the fusion step runs 15 times a second and checks these cases in order:

  1. You. Both glasses sensors read above your calibrated own-voice level, within 3 dB of each other. Your mouth is about 10 cm from them, so your voice arrives about 15–25 dB louder than anyone else's. The caption goes to the "You" bar at the bottom.
  2. A visible speaker. A face's lip-motion score (the variation of mouth opening over 1 s) is ≥ 0.03, and its lips move in time with the sound. The speaker is held at least 0.5 s, and only changes to someone scoring 1.5× higher. The bubble points at that face.
  3. A probable visible speaker. Exactly one face is in the uncertain band (0.015–0.03) and everyone else is still. The bubble points at that face with a dashed tail.
  4. Off-screen. No visible lips are moving. After about 1 s of speech, the voice print is compared with enrolled and session voices, and a match of ≥ 0.5 names the speaker. The side comes from where that person left the frame if that was under 30 s ago; otherwise the louder sound sensor decides, if it's at least 3 dB ahead. Until then the bubble says "Someone" with a side arrow, or sits at the bottom with a "?".
  5. Voice-print harvesting. When case 2 has been confident for 1.5 s of speech, that stretch of voice is added to the person's print for this session. That's what lets case 4 name strangers too.

The in-time check in case 2 compares the rhythm of mouth opening with the rhythm of the sound over the last second, after the calibrated offset. Talking moves the lips with the syllables; smiling, nodding and chewing don't. The dry runs showed it's needed (section 6). When two people talk at once, the caption goes to the higher score. The demo script avoids overlaps.

Captions

  • Speech on and off. Speech starts when Silero's probability passes 0.5 and ends below 0.35. Blips under 250 ms are ignored, and 400 ms of silence ends an utterance.
  • Streaming. Nemotron decodes 560 ms chunks; T-S2 also tries 320 and 1120 ms. It adds punctuation and capitals and reports the language. Draft text shows grey, and final text white.
  • Word times. Every word carries a timestamp, so if the speaker changes mid-sentence, the text is split at the change.
  • Loudness. Your voice is far louder in the glasses mic than anyone else's. Each utterance is level-matched before the ASR sees it.
  • Fallback. faster-whisper large-v3-turbo with whisper_streaming's method, which only shows words that two passes agree on. That's a 1–2 s delay with the same features.
  • Other languages. The sherpa-onnx build exposes Nemotron's language setting, so the engine can use auto-detect or lock to English and Spanish. If Spanish is weak in T-S2, utterances flagged as another language are re-run through Whisper.
  • No noise filter on the caption audio. Modern speech models usually get worse on cleaned-up audio, because the cleanup adds artifacts. DeepFilterNet stays an A/B test for very loud halls only.
  • Layout. A bubble holds at most 2 lines of about 42 characters, older text scrolls off, and it fades 4 s after the last word. The history panel keeps the full transcript.

Learning names

  • Word filter first. Only sentences with an introduction phrase go to the language model: "I'm", "I am", "my name", "call me", "this is", "meet", "me llamo", "soy". That saves a model call on every sentence and cuts false proposals.
  • Structured answer. Ollama must reply in a fixed JSON shape: whether it's an introduction, the name, whose name it is (the speaker's or someone else's), and a confidence. Temperature is 0 and thinking is off. The prompt carries about 8 examples, half of them traps such as "I'm tired", "I'm Sam's sister" and "I'm going to Sam's".
  • Sanity checks. The name must be 1–3 words and not a common word; a stop-list of about 200 words ("fine", "good", "here", "sorry") catches those. Introductions attributed to "You" are ignored, because your own name is known.
  • Confirm by touch. The tag becomes "Sam?" with "tap to confirm", and the right LED glows once. A tap accepts the name for this session only. A hold rejects it. After 10 s with no answer, the proposal expires.
  • Upgrade with consent. The console then offers "Enroll Sam permanently?", which needs Sam's own consent through the same enrollment steps.
  • Touch priority. An active alert takes the tap first (acknowledge), then a pending name. Otherwise a tap does nothing.

Describing strangers

  • Instant color. Once an unknown face has been steady for 1 s, the engine looks at the region below it (the upper body) and finds its main color by clustering the pixels. The tag reads "Person in blue" within about 1 s, with no language model involved.
  • Refined garment. In the background, the qwen3.5 vision model gets the upper-body crop and must answer from fixed lists, enforced by a JSON schema. The tag becomes "Person in blue jacket" about 1–2 s later.
  • Duplicates. If two strangers get the same label, the second adds an accessory or a number ("Person in blue jacket, 2").

Translation

  • Trigger. A final utterance whose language isn't English. The ASR's guess is double-checked by Lingua on the text, because short sentences are easy to mislabel.
  • Engine. The same qwen3.5:4b, told to translate only, at temperature 0. The upgrade is TranslateGemma 4B, a dedicated translation model (3.3 GB), if memory allows. The light fallback is Opus-MT Spanish→English through CTranslate2, which is small and quick on the CPU.
  • Display. While Maya speaks Spanish, Maya's bubble shows the Spanish draft in italics with an "ES" tag. About 0.5–1.2 s after the final, the English replaces it, with the Spanish in small type underneath.
  • Expected languages. The console can set them (English and Spanish, say), which prevents rare mislabels as Portuguese or Italian.
  • One job at a time. Ollama runs one request at a time, so the engine queues jobs by priority: translation, then names, then descriptions.

Sound alerts

Two independent listeners feed one decision.

  • The model. Every 0.5 s, EfficientAT (mn10_as) scores the last 1 s of 32 kHz audio against 527 sound classes. Classes are picked by name (Smoke detector, Fire alarm, Doorbell, Ding-dong, Speech, Music), never by number, because different models number them differently.
  • The rhythm detector. Every 10 ms it tracks tone energy in two bands: 2.8–3.4 kHz, where most alarms sound, and 470–570 Hz, for the low-pitched alarms used in bedrooms. It checks the on/off timing against the two standard patterns. T3 is three 0.5 s beeps with 0.5 s gaps, then 1.5 s off; T4 is four 0.1 s beeps, then 5 s off. It allows ±20% on timing and requires a pure tone, which rules out speech.

  • Output. A red "Smoke alarm · left" banner (amber for the doorbell), the matching light and buzz pattern, and a line in the history.
  • Ending. A tap acknowledges the alert: lights and buzz stop, and the banner reads "Acknowledged". If the sound is still going after 30 s, it alerts again. It clears after 15 s of quiet. Any window in which Attune's own motor was running is ignored, so the buzz can't keep an alarm alive.

The two pages

Lens view

  • Full screen in Chrome on the laptop. Judges watch it, and OBS records it.
  • Each frame is drawn with face tags, speech bubbles with tails to the speaker's mouth, off-screen bubbles docked at the edge with an arrow, the "You" bar, and alert banners.
  • Uses the films' HUD look (colors, type and bubble shapes), so the demo matches them.
  • Bubbles push apart instead of overlapping, and glide rather than jump. Faces under 40 px get a dot instead of a tag.
  • H hides the status strip (fps, mic level, Arduino link, caption delay) for the demo.

Operator console

  • A second Chrome window on the laptop, or panels that slide over the lens view (C console, S speak, Y history). With an external monitor it gets its own screen.
  • Enroll, rename, delete and forget session. Switches for translation, alerts and debug, and a test button for each light and buzz pattern.
  • Calibration wizard, live levels, GPU memory, fps, caption delay, and whether Ollama is warm.
  • An event log with a Mark button for noting problems during rehearsals.
  • Keyboard fallback on the laptop: E enroll, F forget, P pause, H hide status, 1–9 replies.

Speak for me (ElevenLabs)

  • The Speak panel sits on the laptop: a text box, plus preset replies on keys 1–5 ("Nice to meet you", "Can you repeat that?", "One moment", "Thank you", "I read captions, go ahead").
  • Suggested replies. Qwen offers three short replies based on the last few lines, on keys 7–9, so the wearer can answer with one key press.
  • The voice. The engine sends the text to ElevenLabs' low-latency voice and plays it from the laptop's speakers. Pick one preset voice; cloning the wearer's own voice needs their consent and isn't needed for the demo.
  • No double captions. The glasses mic hears the laptop speak. The engine shows the reply as "You (typed)" and ignores mic captions while it plays, plus 0.5 s.
  • Offline fallback. If no audio arrives within 1.5 s (Wi-Fi down, credits used up), Kokoro speaks the reply locally instead.
  • Only the typed text goes to ElevenLabs. Nothing anyone else says leaves the laptop.

Conversation history

  • Storage. One SQLite file on the laptop (data/history.db). Every final caption, translation, typed reply, alert and confirmed name becomes a timestamped row.
  • The history panel (press Y) lists sessions by time. Each one is a scrolling timeline with a color per speaker, and translations and alerts in line. It has a search box, a filter by person, and a "Sounds you missed" list.
  • Deleted after a day. Rows older than 24 hours are deleted when the engine starts and every 10 minutes. Search uses SQLite's built-in full-text index, and the talk-time chart (per person per minute) is a simple query.
  • Never stored: audio, video and face prints.
  • Consent. Names are saved only for people who agreed. Strangers are saved under their clothing label, and "Forget this session" deletes their rows too. ShellHacks is in Florida, which generally requires everyone's consent to record a conversation, and a saved transcript may count. This isn't legal advice.
  • If the history writer fails, captions keep running; rows wait in memory and are written when it's back.

Recording the demo

  • OBS Studio records a Window Capture of the lens view, plus the webcam mic as an Audio Input Capture. Windows lets OBS and the engine read the mic at the same time.
  • Settings: the GPU's NVENC encoder, H.264, 1080p30, quality level (CQP) about 20. Record to MKV with automatic remux to MP4 turned on, so a crash never ruins the file.
  • The engine also writes a session log of every event with timestamps. Replay mode can re-run a whole recorded session (section 7).
  • After the feature freeze, record a clean backup demo video.

Privacy by design

  • Consent first. Only people who tick consent themselves are enrolled, and each consent is saved with the time.
  • Strangers are temporary. They exist only in memory for the session. "Forget this session" wipes their face prints, voice prints, descriptions, name proposals, transcripts and the session log.
  • No sensitive guesses. Descriptions come from fixed clothing and accessory lists. No model that guesses age, gender, emotion or ethnicity is loaded.
  • Local by default. Captions, faces, voices and history stay on the laptop. The only thing that goes online is a reply the wearer types, sent to ElevenLabs. To prove it, turn the laptop's Wi-Fi off mid-demo: everything keeps working, and replies switch to the offline voice.
  • Pause. A double tap pauses all recognition. The HUD and the LED matrix show "Paused".
  • Why it matters. Face prints are biometric data under laws such as Illinois' BIPA, the CCPA and GDPR Article 9, and the EU AI Act restricts biometric identification. Consent, local processing, deletion and no sensitive traits are the right defaults to pitch. This is not legal advice.

Calibration, 10 minutes at the venue

  1. Level. Look at a door frame and rotate the webcam until its edges are vertical in the lens view.
  2. Mic level. Read a sentence, and set the input so peaks land around −6 dBFS.
  3. Your voice. Read a sentence, then have a teammate read one from 1.5 m. The "You" threshold is set halfway between the two, in decibels.
  4. Left/right balance. Play steady noise from the JBL straight ahead at 1.5 m, and trim until both sensors match.
  5. Lip-to-sound offset. Clap three times in view. The engine lines up each clap's sound with the hands meeting, and saves the offset.
  6. Room noise. 30 s of normal hall noise sets the speech and alert baselines.
  7. Face check. Each enrolled teammate stands at 1, 2 and 3 m while the console shows their match scores. If any score is under 0.5, re-enroll under the venue's lighting.

Everything is saved as a venue profile, so a restart doesn't need recalibrating.

Fallback ladder

If something breaks on the day, step down one rung at a time and keep demoing.

  1. Full system.
  2. No language model. Names come from the console only; no translation or garment descriptions.
  3. No rig sensors. The keyboard confirms, and alerts show on screen only.
  4. Seated demo. The webcam on a stand, or the laptop's own camera, running the same software.
  5. The backup video recorded at the feature freeze.

A Chrome-only version, using its on-device speech and translation, could caption without the engine, but building it would cost hours. It stays an idea, not part of the plan.

Judge questions

QuestionAnswer
Does it need the internet?No. Everything runs on this laptop. Only ElevenLabs speaking typed replies is online, and with Wi-Fi off a local voice takes over.
Where is the conversation history kept?In a SQLite file on this laptop. It deletes itself after 24 hours, and Forget removes strangers straight away.
What about privacy?Only people who consent are enrolled. Strangers are remembered for this session only, and described by their clothing. One button forgets the session, and nothing leaves the laptop.
How does it know who's talking?It matches lip movement to the sound, recognizes your own voice by how loud it is at the glasses, and uses voice prints for people off-screen.
How accurate is it?Quote the T-E1 to T-E4 numbers measured at the event.
Is it a smoke alarm?No. It's a supplemental alert for people who can't hear their alarm. It adds light and touch; it doesn't replace strobe alarms.
Why tape a webcam to glasses?The product is the software. The rig stands in for smart glasses' camera, mics, lights and haptics, so the engine can move to real glasses later.
What runs it?Free, open models on the laptop's GPU: NVIDIA's streaming speech model, InsightFace, EfficientAT, and Qwen through Ollama.
What's next?Move the engine to a phone or a small wearable computer, and show the lens view in real AR glasses.
